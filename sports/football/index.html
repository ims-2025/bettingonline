How NFL betting works

Three core markets define an NFL slate: the point spread (the favorite is asked to win by more than X.5 points), the moneyline (pick the winner outright), and the total (over/under on combined points). Sportsbooks publish openers Sunday night for the following week and adjust until kickoff based on action and information.

Beyond these, you'll find player props (passing yards, rush attempts, anytime touchdown), team totals, halftime lines, alternate spreads/totals, futures and Same Game Parlays. The spread is where most volume goes; props are where most edge lives.

Key numbers that move spreads

NFL spreads cluster around margins of victory: 3 (field goal) and 7 (touchdown) are by far the most common, with 4, 6 and 10 also significant. Buying off the 3 (e.g., -3 to -2.5) is one of the most expensive moves in betting; selling onto the 3 (-3.5 to -3) sometimes returns enough juice to be worth it.

Teasers exploit this. A 6-point teaser through 3 and 7 — for example, -8.5 down to -2.5, or +1.5 up to +7.5 — has historically been profitable for sharp bettors when applied to the right games (home favorites, low totals).

Sharp NFL strategy

1. Take early-week numbers. Lines are softest Sunday night through Tuesday before public money sharpens them. If you have an opinion, take it Monday, not Saturday.

2. Shop player props aggressively. The same Patrick Mahomes passing yards line can sit at 268.5, 272.5 and 269.5 across three books. That's a meaningful edge for bettors playing 100+ props a season.

3. Skip the boost trap. Books advertise huge boosts on parlays and SGPs. They're priced with massive holds. Use boosts only on single-leg or two-leg moneylines where the implied probability is genuinely lifted.

4. Track your CLV. If your average price beats the close by 2 cents, you'll be profitable over time even if your record looks rough.
